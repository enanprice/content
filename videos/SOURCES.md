# Sources

One entry per script, copied from each JSON file's `source` field so the claims can be checked in one place.
`evidence` is the script writer's own rating: **strong** (repeated many times), **moderate** (supported, but few or small studies), **mixed** (disputed or unreliable).

## What a pounding heart before an exam means

`videos/exam-nerves.json` · 40s

- **Source:** Jamieson, J. P., Mendes, W. B., Blackstock, E., & Schmader, T. (2010). Turning the knots in your stomach into bows: Reappraising arousal improves performance on the GRE. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 46(1), 208-212.
- **What it found:** Students told before a practice test that feeling keyed up does not hurt performance, and may help it, scored higher on the maths section than a control group, and reported higher maths scores on the real exam one to three months later.
- **Evidence:** mixed
- **Caveat:** A small single study of adult students sitting a US graduate entrance exam. Later studies of the same idea have had uneven results. It is about ordinary pre-exam nerves only; it is not a treatment for anxiety, and the script points anyone who is struggling towards a trusted adult instead of offering advice.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2009.08.015

## Wanting to revise is not a plan

`videos/implementation-intentions.json` · 37s

- **Source:** Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69-119. The technique was introduced in Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans. American Psychologist, 54(7), 493-503.
- **What it found:** Across 94 independent tests, people who made 'if situation X, then I will do Y' plans were more likely to act on their goals than people who only held the goal.
- **Evidence:** strong
- **Caveat:** Most of the 94 studies involved adults and short-term goals, not teenagers revising. The effect in everyday life is likely smaller than the headline average.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1

## Stop doing twenty of the same question

`videos/interleaving.json` · 40s

- **Source:** Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2007). The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning. Instructional Science, 35(6), 481-498.
- **What it found:** Students who practised different types of maths problem mixed together scored lower during practice but much higher on a test one week later than students who practised one type at a time.
- **Evidence:** moderate
- **Caveat:** The evidence for mixing is strongest in maths, where choosing the right method is part of the problem. Results in other subjects are less consistent, and the script says so.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8

## It doesn't take 21 days

`videos/motivation-vs-habit.json` · 41s

- **Source:** Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998-1009.
- **What it found:** Volunteers repeating a chosen daily behaviour took a median of 66 days for it to feel automatic, with individuals ranging from 18 to 254 days. Missing a single day did not noticeably affect the process.
- **Evidence:** moderate
- **Caveat:** A small study: 96 volunteers started and the 66-day figure rests on 39 of them. The longest times are projections past the 84 days the study ran. It has not been directly replicated. The '21 days' figure is usually traced to Maxwell Maltz's 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics, which was not a habit study.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674

## Is your phone distracting you when you're not using it?

`videos/phone-distraction.json` · 40s

- **Source:** Ward, A. F., Duke, K., Gneezy, A., & Bos, M. W. (2017). Brain drain: The mere presence of one's own smartphone reduces available cognitive capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, 2(2), 140-154. Second study cited: Stothart, C., Mitchum, A., & Yehnert, C. (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 41(4), 893-897.
- **What it found:** Ward et al. reported lower scores on thinking tests when participants' phones were on the desk rather than in another room. Stothart et al. reported that receiving a notification, without responding to it, increased errors on an attention task.
- **Evidence:** mixed
- **Caveat:** The 'brain drain' result is disputed: a pre-registered replication (Ruiz Pardo & Minda, 2022, Acta Psychologica) did not find it, and meta-analyses disagree about whether the effect exists and how big it is. The notification study is a single experiment. The script says this out loud.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1086/691462

## Why the Pomodoro technique really works

`videos/pomodoro.json` · 39s

- **Source:** Cirillo, F. (2006). The Pomodoro Technique. Self-published; later editions by Currency (2018).
- **What it found:** Describes the 25-minute work / 5-minute break method. It is a practitioner's method, not a research finding.
- **Evidence:** mixed
- **Caveat:** The script's claim that the method works mainly by making it easier to start is a plausible argument, not an established result. Controlled studies of the technique itself are few and small.

## More time doesn't mean better work

`videos/procrastination.json` · 40s

- **Source:** Ariely, D., & Wertenbroch, K. (2002). Procrastination, deadlines, and performance: Self-control by precommitment. Psychological Science, 13(3), 219-224.
- **What it found:** In a proofreading experiment, people given evenly spaced deadlines found the most errors and were least late; people with one final deadline did worst; people who set their own deadlines came in between.
- **Evidence:** moderate
- **Caveat:** This is one well-known study with small groups of adults, not a body of repeated findings. One of its authors, Dan Ariely, has since had other papers retracted over data problems; I am not aware of concerns raised about this paper, but check before publishing.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00441

## Rereading is not revising

`videos/retrieval-practice.json` · 38s

- **Source:** Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249-255.
- **What it found:** Students who read a passage and then tested themselves recalled more of it two days and one week later than students who reread it, even though the rereaders did slightly better after five minutes.
- **Evidence:** strong
- **Caveat:** The original study used university students and prose passages. The effect has since been repeated many times with other ages and materials, which is why it is rated strong.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x

## The late-night revision trade-off

`videos/sleep-and-memory.json` · 40s

- **Source:** Gais, S., Lucas, B., & Born, J. (2006). Sleep after learning aids memory recall. Learning & Memory, 13(3), 259-262. Wider review: Rasch, B., & Born, J. (2013). About sleep's role in memory. Physiological Reviews, 93(2), 681-766.
- **What it found:** People who slept within a few hours of learning word material recalled more of it later than people who stayed awake that night, even after everyone had a recovery night's sleep before the test.
- **Evidence:** moderate
- **Caveat:** The 2006 experiments were small lab studies using word lists. The wider research consistently links sleep with memory, but that is not the same as showing a set amount of sleep adds marks in an exam. The script gives no advice on how much sleep anyone needs.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.132106

## Why cramming is slow

`videos/spaced-repetition.json` · 38s

- **Source:** Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354-380.
- **What it found:** A review pooling 317 experiments found that spreading study over separate sessions led to better later recall than the same amount of study in one block.
- **Evidence:** strong
- **Caveat:** Most of the experiments used simple verbal material such as word lists and facts. The best gap depends on how far away the test is, so 'two days' in the script is a reasonable example, not a magic number.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354

## Should you always revise in the same place?

`videos/study-environment.json` · 40s

- **Source:** Smith, S. M., Glenberg, A., & Bjork, R. A. (1978). Environmental context and human memory. Memory & Cognition, 6(4), 342-353. Meta-analysis: Smith, S. M., & Vela, E. (2001). Environmental context-dependent memory: A review and meta-analysis. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 8(2), 203-220.
- **What it found:** Students who studied a word list in two different rooms recalled more of it when tested in a new room than students who studied it twice in the same room.
- **Evidence:** mixed
- **Caveat:** The 2001 meta-analysis found that place-dependent memory effects are real on average but modest, and individual studies often fail to find them. The original task was memorising word lists, not revising a subject. The script says the effect is small and unreliable.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03197465
